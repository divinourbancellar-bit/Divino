The Grape That Waits: A Story and a Poem for Nero d'Avola

18 April 2026 From the cellar 8 min read

On top of my grandmother's wardrobe, lying on its side on a folded tea towel, in a bedroom that reached forty degrees every August, there was one bottle of red wine. It was there for eleven years. I know it was eleven because I was seven when I first noticed it and eighteen when it came down, and I spent a decent portion of the intervening period thinking about it.

It was, I should say immediately, in the worst possible place. Hot, bright, upright half the year because someone kept knocking it. If you wanted to design an environment for destroying a bottle of wine you would build my grandmother's bedroom and then add the wardrobe.

She knew this. She was not a stupid woman. She simply had a different theory about what the bottle was for.

The occasions that were not good enough

The bottle was being saved. That much was understood in the house the way the location of the fuse box was understood. What was never established, in eleven years, was what it was being saved for.

My uncle got married. Not that.

My cousin was born, and then my cousin's brother. Not that either — though on the second one she went as far as taking the bottle down, holding it, reading the label as though it were a letter, and putting it back.

My grandfather recovered from something the family only ever referred to as “the business in March.” She cooked for nineteen people. She opened four bottles of something perfectly ordinary from the shop on the corner. The wardrobe bottle did not move.

I asked her about it once, directly, when I was about fourteen and going through a period of asking adults direct questions to see what would happen. I said: what is it for?

She was doing something at the sink and did not turn around. She said, “Per quando serve.” For when it's needed.

Which I found, at fourteen, deeply unsatisfying, and which I now think is one of the more complete things anyone has ever said to me.

The Tuesday

It came down on a Tuesday in November. Nothing had happened. This is the part I want to be clear about, because every time I tell this story someone tries to supply a reason, and there wasn't one. It was not a birthday. Nobody had died. Nobody had recovered from anything. It was a Tuesday in November, unseasonably warm, and she had been out to buy bread and had come back in and put the bread down.

I was in the kitchen doing something I have entirely forgotten. She went into the bedroom. I heard the chair being dragged over — she was eighty-one and getting on the chair was, by then, an event with its own sound. She came back with the bottle and the tea towel.

She opened it at the counter. She did not sit down. She did not call anyone. She poured two glasses, gave me one — I was eighteen, this was legal and also, in that house, not a question anyone would have thought to ask — and we stood in the kitchen and drank it.

It was, in strictly technical terms, not good.

I want to be honest about this, because the version of this story where the wine is miraculously perfect is a story about wine, and this is not really a story about wine. It had gone slightly brown at the edge. It had lost most of its fruit. There was a dried-out quality to it, a sort of thinness, that eleven years on top of a wardrobe in a hot room will do to anything. A sommelier would have poured it away.

But it was not dead. That is the strange part, and it is the reason I ended up doing this for a living. Underneath the tiredness there was still that specific Nero d'Avola thing — a dark, sour-edged, almost savoury note, like plum skin and something faintly medicinal — holding on, refusing to be finished. A grape from the hottest, driest corner of a hot dry island, bred by nine hundred years of weather to hang on to its acidity when everything around it gives up.

It had waited eleven years in the worst room in the house and it still had something to say.

“Per quando serve.” For when it's needed. It took me twenty years to work out that she had not been waiting for something big. She had been waiting until she wanted it.

She finished her glass, washed it, and put it on the rack. Then she said the only thing she ever said about the entire eleven-year business, which was: “Well. That's that.”

And she started on the bread.

The poem

I wrote this some years later, after a September harvest, sitting on a crate. It is the only poem I have ever finished, which I mention so that you calibrate your expectations correctly.

The Grape That Waits Nothing on this hill is in a hurry. The sun takes all of July to say one word and says it again in August, in case the stones weren't listening. The vine is thirty years old and unimpressed. Each February a man cuts it back to almost nothing without explaining himself, and each February it agrees. Black fruit. Thick skin. A sourness held in reserve the way a family holds a story — not secret, exactly. Just not yet. We pick in the last serious heat and the crates go down the hill in a van with one working door. Then the waiting, which is the real work: the dark, the wood, the glass, the long arithmetic of a cellar where nothing appears to happen and everything does. My grandmother kept a bottle eleven years for an occasion that never came, and drank it on a Tuesday, standing up, in the kitchen, having understood at last that the occasion was the Tuesday. So open it. Whatever you are saving it for is already here — in its ordinary clothes, hoping you'll notice.

Now the useful part: what Nero d'Avola actually is

Having got the sentiment out of my system, here is the wine writing.

Nero d'Avola — “the black of Avola,” after the town in the south-eastern corner of the island, near Syracuse — is Sicily's most widely planted red grape and the closest thing the island has to a signature. You will also see it called Calabrese on older labels, which has nothing to do with Calabria and is generally thought to be a mangling of a Sicilian phrase meaning, more or less, “from Avola.”

For most of its history nobody outside Sicily drank it as itself. It was a blending wine: deep, dark, high in alcohol, shipped north in bulk to give colour and body to thinner wines made in cooler places that would never have admitted to it. An entire island's worth of grapes, anonymised. The shift to serious single-variety bottlings is comparatively recent — a matter of a few decades — which is why so much Nero d'Avola on supermarket shelves is still made to a volume mindset, and why a good one surprises people so much.

What to expect in the glass

  • Colour: deep, opaque, purple-black when young. It stains everything. Do not wear the good shirt.
  • Aroma: black cherry and plum first, then liquorice, dried herbs, tobacco, and often a dark peppery note.
  • Palate: full-bodied and warm, with real tannin — but the thing that separates good from mediocre is a sour, savoury lift underneath the fruit. That acidity is why the grape can be planted somewhere this hot and still make a wine that tastes alive rather than stewed.
  • Finish: long, dry, faintly bitter — that same bitter turn you find right through Sicilian food.

Ageing — honestly

A well-made Nero d'Avola from a serious producer will improve for five to ten years, and the best examples go further. The tannin softens, the fruit darkens toward dried fig and prune, and the tobacco-and-liquorice side comes forward.

An inexpensive supermarket bottle will not do this. It will simply get tired. If you want to keep something, keep something that was built to be kept, and keep it lying down, in the dark, somewhere that stays cool and does not swing wildly in temperature.

In other words: not on top of a wardrobe. I feel obliged to say this, having spent a thousand words being moved by exactly that.

What to drink it with

Nero d'Avola is a food wine and slightly awkward on its own — the tannin has nothing to grip. Give it fat, salt or char and it becomes a different wine entirely.

  • Grilled lamb or beef, anything with a proper crust on it
  • Caponata — the sweet-and-sour aubergine that is Sicily's whole personality on a plate
  • Sausage with fennel seed
  • Aged pecorino or a hard ragusano
  • Anything baked with tomato and aubergine and too much cheese

Serve it at around 16 to 18 °C — which in a Sicilian house in summer means twenty minutes in the fridge. Room temperature is a phrase invented in a colder country and it has ruined more red wine here than heat ever did.

One last thing worth knowing: in Cerasuolo di Vittoria, Sicily's only DOCG, Nero d'Avola is blended with Frappato, and the result is one of the most surprising red wines in Italy — the weight of the one, the perfume and lift of the other. If you have decided you do not like Nero d'Avola, that is very often the bottle that reopens the negotiation. We usually have one at the cellar.

Which brings us back to the wardrobe

Somebody asks me, most weeks, what they should do with a bottle they are saving. They mean it as a technical question — how long, what temperature, will it hold.

The technical answer is above. The real answer is that most people who are saving a bottle are not saving it for a date, they are saving it for a version of their life that they are waiting to arrive in: the house finished, the job settled, the right people around the right table. And that version keeps not arriving, and the wine keeps sitting there, and the sitting there starts to feel like the point.

It isn't the point. My grandmother worked that out at eighty-one, on a warm Tuesday in November, with the bread on the counter and nobody there but me. She got about four years out of the discovery. I would like you to get more.

Open the bottle. If you would rather open it with someone who will tell you what it is doing, the long table is right here, and there is almost always something dark and sour-edged and Sicilian on it.
